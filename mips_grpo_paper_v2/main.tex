\title{From Counts to Paths: Multiplicity-Corrected Inverse Probability
Scaling for Reward-Proportional Sampling in Large DAGs}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Many scientific generators should represent a distribution of useful outcomes,
not return one reward maximizer. Count-based inverse probability scaling (IPS)
targets reward-proportional sampling only while outcomes repeat inside a sampled
group. When the outcome space is large, the count estimate becomes constant and
the correction degenerates to the original reward. Replacing counts by the
known trajectory probability is also wrong in a directed acyclic graph (DAG):
outcomes reachable by more paths are over-weighted. We introduce the path
weight \(R(x)q(\tau\mid x)/P_F(\tau)\), where \(q\) is any normalized reverse
policy. The path contributions sum to \(R(x)\) for every terminal, independent
of path multiplicity and without requiring repeats. A controlled hypergrid
shows both sides of the count boundary: count IPS recovers all four modes when
the group is larger than the terminal space, but collapses to one when there
are 128 terminals per group sample; MIPS-GRPO recovers all modes in both. We
then observe the same support-preservation signature in two applications. At
the headline 27-taxon phylogenetic scale, the recoverable MIPS-GRPO artifact
contains 999,987 distinct signatures in one million draws and has pathwise
probability--reward correlation \(r=0.977\). This result is preliminary while
the matched multi-seed comparison finishes. In an
interim full-space molecular-synthesis experiment, MIPS-GRPO produces 99.4\%
distinct valid samples while GRPO and count IPS assign at least 98.6\% of their
mass to one molecule. The applications support the mechanism beyond the toy
setting while revealing a quality--diversity tradeoff against flow-based
baselines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Drug design, program synthesis, and Bayesian inference often have many useful
answers. For such problems, returning only the highest-reward terminal is less
useful than representing the full reward-weighted set. The desired target is
\begin{equation}
 p^*(x)=\frac{R(x)}{Z}, \qquad Z=\sum_x R(x).
 \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
Expected-return maximization does not target Eq.~\eqref{eq:target}; it rewards
moving probability toward the best terminal and therefore encourages
outcome-level collapse. IPS cancels the terminal-frequency factor in the
policy gradient by dividing reward by terminal probability
\citep{sinha2026ips}.

Count IPS-GRPO estimates terminal probability from repeats inside a group of
\(G\) samples. If the effective terminal space \(K\) is much larger than
\(G\), almost every observed terminal appears once and \(\hat p(x)=1/G\).
Group normalization removes the common factor, leaving essentially the GRPO
update.

A sampled trajectory has a known probability \(P_F(\tau)\), suggesting the
weight \(R(x)/P_F(\tau)\). This works in a tree but not a DAG. If terminal
\(x\) has \(m(x)\) construction paths, that weight assigns it total mass
\(m(x)R(x)\), confounding reward with reachability. This is central in both
applications studied here: a phylogenetic tree has many merge orders and a
molecule may have many synthesis trajectories.

Our paper follows a three-act evidence chain. First, a controlled hypergrid
isolates when counting works and when it fails, while making path multiplicity
exactly known. Second, Bayesian phylogenetic inference tests a combinatorial
application where the outcome space rapidly becomes intractable. Third,
full-space molecular synthesis tests a chemically structured generation DAG
and a different flow-based baseline.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
 \item We identify \(K/G\), the effective number of terminals per group
 sample, as the practical boundary for within-group count IPS.
 \item We derive a count-free trajectory weight that is unbiased at the
 terminal level for any finite DAG.
 \item On one reward landscape, we show count IPS succeeding at
 \(K/G=0.25\) and failing at \(K/G=128\), while MIPS-GRPO recovers all modes
 in both regimes.
 \item We demonstrate the same non-collapse signature at the 27-taxon scale in
 Bayesian phylogenetic inference and in full-space molecular synthesis.
 \item We keep the evidence boundary explicit: the current 27-taxon result is
 a preliminary self-sampled pathwise diagnostic pending its matched multi-seed
 replacement, and the molecular comparison is one seed at an interim
 500-update checkpoint.
\end{itemize}

\section{Multiplicity-corrected IPS}

Let a forward policy generate trajectory
\(\tau=(s_0,a_1,\ldots,a_T,s_T)\) and terminal \(x=s_T\). Its path
probability is known,
\begin{equation}
 P_F(\tau)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}P_F(a_t\mid s_{t-1}),
\end{equation}
but the terminal probability is a sum over paths,
\begin{equation}
 P_F(x)=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T(x)}P_F(\tau).
 \label{eq:marginal}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:marginal} becomes intractable in large DAGs.

\subsection{Why naive path IPS is wrong}

For \(w_{\mathrm{naive}}(\tau)=R(x)/P_F(\tau)\), the expected weighted mass
assigned to terminal \(x\) is
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T(x)}P_F(\tau)w_{\mathrm{naive}}(\tau)
 =|\mathcal T(x)|R(x).
\end{equation}
The desired reward is multiplied by path count. This is structural bias, not
merely high variance.

\subsection{Reverse allocation across paths}

Let \(q(\tau\mid x)\) be any normalized distribution over paths terminating
at \(x\): \(\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T(x)}q(\tau\mid x)=1\). Define
\begin{equation}
 w(\tau)=\frac{R(x)q(\tau\mid x)}{P_F(\tau)}.
 \label{eq:mips}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T(x)}P_F(\tau)w(\tau)
 =R(x)\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal T(x)}q(\tau\mid x)=R(x).
 \label{eq:unbiased}
\end{equation}
Normalization of \(q\), not a particular learned model, establishes
terminal-level correctness. A uniform backward policy is valid. If
\(q(\tau\mid x)=P_F(\tau\mid x)\), the weight becomes the ideal
\(R(x)/P_F(x)\) and is constant across paths to the same terminal. Learning a
reverse model may reduce variance, but that is an empirical benefit rather
than a correctness requirement.

\subsection{Policy update and relationship to GFlowNets}

For each on-policy batch we sample trajectories, score them with a frozen
reverse policy, form
\begin{equation}
 \log w_i=\log R(x_i)+\log q(\tau_i\mid x_i)-\log P_F(\tau_i),
\end{equation}
normalize weights into advantages, and take a path-level PPO update. When
\(q=q_\phi\) is fitted, maximum likelihood on observed reverse edges is
performed only after scoring the batch. This freeze--score--fit order prevents
the batch from first raising its own reverse probability and then its weight.

GFlowNets also target reward-proportional terminal distributions
\citep{bengio2021gflownet,bengio2023foundations}. Trajectory balance uses a
backward policy and learned partition function in a squared balance residual
\citep{malkin2022tb}; MIPS-GRPO uses the backward allocation inside an
importance weight and updates the forward policy with PPO.

\section{Controlled proof: where counting works and fails}

\subsection{Hypergrid and two regimes}

We use a two-dimensional hypergrid with \(H^2\) terminals and four symmetric
high-reward modes. Starting at \((0,0)\), each action increments one coordinate
or terminates. The reward landscape follows \citet{sinha2026ips}, with
\(R_0=0.1\), \(R_1=0.5\), and \(R_2=2.0\). Because
\(p(x)\propto R(x)\) is enumerable, we measure exact empirical \(\ell_1\)
distance to target, modes recovered, and terminal coverage.

At \(H=8,G=256\), there are 64 terminals and repeats are plentiful
(\(K/G=0.25\)). At \(H=64,G=32\), there are 4096 terminals and repeats are
rare (\(K/G=128\)). The latter is also a multiplicity stress test: terminal
\((a,b)\) has \(\binom{a+b}{a}\) paths, and the four equal-reward modes differ
in path count by roughly 24 orders of magnitude.

\subsection{Results}

When counts are informative, count IPS-GRPO lowers \(\ell_1\) from
\(1.768\) for GRPO to \(\SmallGridCountLone\) and recovers all modes. This is
the positive control: count IPS works when its estimator has repeats.
MIPS-GRPO also recovers all modes and reaches \(\SmallGridMIPSLone\), roughly
four times lower error than count IPS.

When counts are sparse, GRPO and count IPS-GRPO each recover one mode, with
\(\ell_1=\LargeGridGRPOLone\) and \(\LargeGridCountLone\). MIPS-GRPO visits
4056 of 4096 terminals, recovers all modes, and reaches
\(\ell_1=\LargeGridMIPSLone\). GFlowNet TB also recovers all modes and reaches
\(\LargeGridTBLone\). We do not claim a difference between MIPS-GRPO and TB in
this single-seed comparison; their results are effectively tied.

 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figure4_hypergrid_failure_diagnostics.png}
 \caption{\textbf{The missing modes are collapse, not slow exploration.}
 Left: additional samples do not reveal modes missed by GRPO or count IPS.

Drawing up to 50,000 samples from GRPO and count IPS does not reveal missing
modes, while both multiplicity-aware methods find all four within roughly
\(10^2\) samples (Figure~\ref{fig:hypergrid-diagnostics}). The collapsed
policies nevertheless achieve the highest possible mean reward: they succeed
at expected-return maximization while failing at distribution matching.

\section{Application I: Bayesian phylogenetic inference}

A phylogenetic tree is built by repeatedly merging subtrees. The same terminal
tree has many merge orders, making multiplicity intrinsic. We use the
27-taxon DS1 benchmark with positive shifted log likelihood as terminal reward.
A terminal signature contains both topology and branch lengths. PhyloGFN is
the closest task-specific trajectory-balance baseline
\citep{zhou2024phylogfn}.

The current recoverable MIPS-GRPO artifact is a one-million-draw evaluation at
the scale we ultimately care about. It contains 999,987 distinct signatures
(99.9987\% non-repetition), with linear and log--log probability--reward
correlations of 0.977 on its sampled support. Thus the method is not merely
wandering through the large space: the mass assigned to visited outcomes
tracks reward while retaining essentially maximal empirical support.

\section{Application II: full-space molecular synthesis}

We test a chemically structured construction DAG using the sEH proxy task. The
available matched checkpoint has 500 policy updates (50,000 oracle calls), seed
0, and 5,000 independent final-policy samples per method. We compare GRPO,
count IPS-GRPO, MIPS-GRPO, and RGFN. This result is explicitly interim until
the 2,000-update matched evaluation and additional seeds are complete.

GRPO returns 3 unique molecules and assigns 98.6\% of its mass to one molecule;
count IPS returns 2 and assigns 99.96\% to one. MIPS-GRPO returns 4,923 unique
valid molecules (99.4\% non-repetition), assigns 0.081\% to its most common
molecule, and finds three separated high-proxy modes. RGFN is also diverse and
has higher mean proxy, but repeats more. The honest conclusion is a
quality--diversity tradeoff: MIPS-GRPO strongly prevents collapse, while RGFN
currently has better average quality.

\section{What the combined evidence supports}

The controlled experiment supplies the proof: as repeats disappear, count IPS
loses its correction, while a normalized reverse allocation removes both the
repeat requirement and multiplicity bias. The applications add external
validity. Phylogenetics reproduces the support failure in a domain with
enormous path multiplicity. Molecular synthesis reproduces terminal collapse
in a different state space and against a different flow-based baseline. The
common result is support preservation; exact superiority to TB or RGFN is not
established by the current evidence.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}

The large-grid stress test has one seed, and the molecular result is one seed
at an interim checkpoint. The grid regimes change both \(H\) and \(G\); a
sweep in \(K/G\) would locate the transition more precisely. The present
27-taxon figure is a legacy self-sampled diagnostic: its MIPS-GRPO artifact is
recoverable, but the old GFlowNet checkpoint is not, and neither method is
evaluated on a shared fixed tree set. The running matched multi-seed experiment
must replace it before submission. Equation~\eqref{eq:mips} assumes on-policy
data, and replay requires an additional correction. We assume positive
terminal rewards. The appendix 10-taxon ablation finds nearly identical
effective sample size for fitted and uniform reverse policies, so the practical
value of learning \(q_\phi\) remains open.

Within those limits, MIPS provides a count-free answer to reward-proportional
optimization when outcomes do not repeat and many trajectories reach one
terminal. The toy task shows why it works; phylogenetics and molecular
synthesis show why the problem matters.
